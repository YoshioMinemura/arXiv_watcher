Tags: LLM agents, jailbreaking, optimization, zeroth-order methods, first-order methods

Abstract Large Language Model (LLM) agents are vulnerable to adversarial inputs that induce attacker-specified malicious behaviors, commonly referred to as jailbreaking. Existing optimization-based jailbreak attacks primarily employ first-order (FO) methods that leverage gradient information or zeroth-order (ZO) methods that rely on function-value feedback. However, their complementarity and optimization ordering remain underexplored. We systematically investigate four strategies: pure ZO, pure FO, ZO->FO, and FO->ZO, and develop a ZO-FO framework that explores adversarial prefixes through ZO optimization and refines their corresponding suffixes through FO optimization. To coordinate the two processes, we introduce Pair-aware optimization, which retains multiple prefix candidates, conditionally refines their suffixes and selects among the resulting prefix-suffix pairs. We further investigate agent jailbreaking from a more general stochastic-generation perspective, where the same adversarial input can induce diverse reasoning trajectories and actions that a single greedy output cannot capture. Accordingly, we adopt consistent stochastic decoding during both optimization and evaluation. Experiments across malicious-instruction and malicious-environment benchmarks demonstrate that hybrid optimization improves attack effectiveness over single-optimizer methods, while optimization order substantially influences performance.

Tags: LLM agents, Markov decision process, policy optimization, tool use, cost reduction

Abstract A general observation in agentic workflows, especially bounded ones, is that the decision space of the LLM, when we separate a decision from generation, starts approaching saturation: the agent meets the same states again and again, and after enough conversations it stops meeting new ones. LLMs generally don't treat these decisions as an optimization problem and instead re-derive them from their context/prompt on every turn. We show that treating decision making within reasonable bounds as an optimization problem, solving it once, and reusing the solved decisions can often make an agent faster, cheaper and more reliable. Our approach, which we call Discretize, Then Decide, starts from data such as the workflow's own logs or, when none exists, runs of the LLM agent itself. It reads each conversation into discrete states and estimates the probability of each outcome and the cost of each move, until no new states appear and the probabilities stop changing. It then solves the resulting Markov decision process. The solution runs as a controller around the model, sending moves whose content is fixed in the state, such as a tool call or a templated reply, and any state it hasn't seen is sent back to the LLM. We call an LLM run this way a Bellman language model (BLM), after Richard Bellman's equation for the best move in each state. On $τ^2$-bench telecom, the controller with no model solves 79% of tasks. As a BLM, Qwen3.8-27B, an open model, goes from 29% to 96% with 93% fewer model calls. For Claude Opus 5.5, which solves 96% of tasks on its own, running it as a BLM cuts model calls and cost per task by about 98% and lets it solve 2 more tasks. On InventoryBench, the solved policy with no model beats every published method. Where the hard part is writing content rather than choosing moves, such as resolving SymPy issues, few decisions can be solved in advance, and a BLM helps little.
