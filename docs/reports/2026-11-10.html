Tags: large language models, mathematical reasoning, self-correction, verification, bidirectional framework

Abstract Large language models (LLMs) have made substantial progress in mathematical reasoning but still produce incorrect solutions to complex problems, indicating the need for more reliable self-correction mechanisms. Mathematical self-correction typically identifies errors through verification, which can proceed backward or forward. The two directions provide complementary verification capabilities. Backward verification checks a candidate answer against the original constraints, bypassing complex intermediate reasoning, but cannot ensure correct reasoning or objective completeness. Forward verification follows the problem conditions and current solution, enabling it to locate reasoning errors and assess objective completeness, but may overlook key constraints in complex solution chains. We propose BiVerify, a dynamically scheduled framework for bidirectional mathematical verification. BiVerify begins with backward verification to screen the answer against the original constraints, followed by forward verification of the reasoning process; subsequent direction switching, state resetting, and re-verification are governed by verification outcomes. Upon failure, the system revises the candidate or verification flow until the same candidate passes both directions. We also introduce shared mathematical tools that separate logical reasoning from precise computation. We systematically evaluate BiVerify on five challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks using multiple mainstream DeepSeek, GPT, and Gemini models. BiVerify exceeds the strongest representative public baseline evaluated in this work by 13.5 percentage points in average accuracy, with the margin reaching 31.2 percentage points on GPT. Further analysis shows that BiVerify's overall performance exceeds that of either verification direction alone.

Tags: KV cache compression, LLM inference, attention approximation, memory offloading, long-context

Abstract Long-context applications, long-lived agents, and multi-agent systems require large language models (LLMs) to process and retain extensive information. In these settings, the KV cache can exceed GPU high-bandwidth memory (HBM) capacity, necessitating offloading to host memory, where CPU-to-GPU transfers can dominate inference latency. While KV cache compression can reduce data movement, query-agnostic compression that guarantees accurate attention for arbitrary queries is extremely challenging, if not impossible. We introduce VerKV, a hybrid approach that provides $(ε, δ)$ guarantees for approximating attention on any given query. VerKV maintains a compact set of query-agnostic pivotal tokens on the GPU, together with a token-level sketch of the full KV cache, while retaining the complete cache in CPU memory. We introduce a novel stochastic sampling procedure, PoissonComp, to select the pivotal tokens. At inference time, our proposed KVFetch algorithm uses the pivotal tokens and the sketch to estimate the relative error of the resulting attention computation. If the estimate satisfies a user-specified $(ε, δ)$ tolerance, inference proceeds without accessing host memory. Otherwise, VerKV performs query-aware retrieval to fetch additional tokens from the CPU, refining the attention computation to meet the required tolerance. Thus, VerKV enables attention with bounded approximation error while minimizing CPU-to-GPU transfers. We show that PoissonComp outperforms state-of-the-art approaches to KV cache compression. Combined with KVFetch, it achieves near-dense performance (90-100% of dense quality at 32K context length, averaged over RULER and LOFT), using Qwen3-4B, Qwen3.5-9B and Ministral-3-8B, while reducing the GPU memory footprint by 8-32x and keeping CPU-to-GPU memory fetches within 2-23% of the cache per generated token, depending on the model.

Abstract Aligning two models without paired data requires a distance that both share, yet existing methods use Euclidean or spherical geometry. This is either an arbitrary choice or imposed by their loss function. We show that, under a precise form of the manifold hypothesis, the pullback of the data metric gives such a distance for any two models of one data-generating process. This holds whether the models are encoders, decoders or both, and whatever their architecture, loss, prior or training data. The alignment map preserves this distance and the distribution of representations, and is identifiable if and only if the data distribution breaks every symmetry of the data manifold. We show that this symmetry breaking holds generically. Identifiability makes the alignment coupling the unique optimum of Gromov--Wasserstein and of its weighted combinations with a linear program. The linear program alone has the same optimum if almost every data point has a distinct distribution of distances. The coupling, and with it the alignment map, is recovered exactly from permuted samples, even without identifiability, and consistently from independent ones. Our method, Neural Gromov--Monge Alignment (NGMA), uses no pairs or labels and outperforms every unpaired and label-informed baseline. It relies on pullback distances to align image models with different priors and single-cell models trained on disjoint cells, where Euclidean distances fail.
