Tags: LLM, neural alignment, low-rank regression, language processing

Abstract Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as computational models of human language processing, yet it remains unclear which components of their representations drive alignment with neural activity. Here, we use ridge reduced-rank regression to identify the brain-predictive subspace of LLM representations: the representational directions that support linear prediction of neural responses. Across three naturalistic language-listening datasets spanning intracranial electrophysiology and fMRI, and six model conditions spanning autoregressive, bidirectional masked, and randomly initialized language models, we find that full linear encoding performance can be recovered using only 1-3% of the original embedding dimensionality. These low-dimensional subspaces generalize across held-out participants and, to a lesser extent, across datasets, suggesting that neural predictivity is concentrated in a compact and partially shared representational space. We next compare these brain-predictive directions with the principal axes of variation in the embeddings. Principal component analysis requires substantially more dimensions to recover neural prediction, demonstrating that the directions most useful for predicting neural activity are distinct from those capturing the most overall variance within LLM representations themselves. Finally, characterizing the learned subspaces allows us to surface interpretable features that are overrepresented in neural activity above and beyond the LLM representations. Together, these results show that LLM-brain alignment is supported by a small, structured subset of model representations and provides a framework for better understanding exactly why these representations align. Tags: safety alignment, multi-turn dialogue, reinforcement learning, LLM defense, prompt safety

Abstract Safety alignment has enabled large language models to reliably refuse harmful requests directly, yet the same request distributed across a multi-turn conversation still succeeds far too often. This brittleness has a mechanistic root: Alignment is shallow, with safety behavior concentrated in a model's first few output tokens, and existing defenses inherit the weakness, supervising the surface form of the response without ever giving the model an explicit safety decision to learn. We take the opposite view of shallowness: if a short span determines the trajectory of an entire response, that span is the highest-leverage place to intervene, and the defender can occupy it first. Across two model families and fourteen benchmarks spanning safety, utility, and helpfulness, our defenders achieve the strongest multi-turn safety in a controlled comparison, maintaining their lead in safety while preserving utility at a modest cost to helpfulness. A per-token KL analysis and interventional probes confirm why this works: training concentrates its effect on the brief deliberation that sets a response's trajectory, and the decision committed there is a causal control point. The very shallowness attackers exploit becomes the mechanism of defense.

Tags: LLM watermarking, speculative decoding, text generation, copula, cryptography

Abstract We present TextSeal2, a distortion-free watermark for large language models that keeps its signal under speculative decoding: tokens that replace rejected drafts remain watermarked with the same keys, and the acceptance rate is unchanged. It also preserves output diversity and adds no measurable inference latency. Its predecessor, TextSeal, restores diversity in the Gumbel-max scheme by routing each token to one of two keys via a coin flip. This is suboptimal because the detector averages the scores from both keys without knowing which key was used. TextSeal2 instead merges two or more keys into a single virtual key and couples it to fresh noise through AutoMark's Gaussian copula. Any number of keys, including publicly shared keys, can be merged this way without diluting detection. Under speculative decoding, TextSeal2 verifies keyed drafts with one more exponential race, run on the leftovers of the draft races. Abstract On-policy Self-distillation (OPSD) has proven effective for post-training large language models by constructing a teacher conditioned on privileged references. Abstract Unlearning concerns the task of removing the influence of a sensitive portion of the training data, such as protected, copyrighted or private text, from a model without retraining it from scratch. While unlearning requests happen continuously during a model's lifetime, the vast majority of LLM unlearning methods are ill-suited for iterated removal, yielding steep decay in forgetting and utility upon sequential unlearning. Tags: multi-agent systems, personalized learning, adaptive learning, large language models

Abstract Large language models support personalized tutoring, content generation, and adaptive learning paths. However, capability development requires these resources and interactions to form a coherent learning process that evolves with the learner. A central challenge is to align goals, activities, and assessment while using learning evidence to decide what should follow. We introduce Human-Participatory Multi-Agent Organization and instantiate it in Agentic University (AU). In AU, learners participate as organization members and work with specialized agents to maintain a revisable Capability Program. The program connects personal goals, learning activities across modalities, and layered assessment, while learner feedback and performance guide subsequent support. Our evaluation shows advantages in program organization, assessment design, and revision quality. Users achieved larger mean assessment gains (12.19 versus 5.61 points on a 50-point scale) and reported stronger cognitive support. This work contributes an organizational approach to adaptive learning that makes learner participation a continuing basis for multi-agent coordination. The Agentic University project is available at \href{https://github.com/AgenticOrgLab/AgenticUniversity.git}.

Tags: formal reasoning, world model, Lean 4, category theory, game solving

Abstract AI has made remarkable progress in mathematics, solving challenging problems with machine-checkable proofs in Lean, yet typically relying on predefined mathematical definitions and formal specifications. This raises a natural question: can it reason rigorously about an unknown world? We introduce the Algebraic World Model (AWM), a framework that turns interaction with unfamiliar environments into explicit mathematical models supporting formal reasoning. AWM explores the environment and develops executable Python hypotheses, continually testing and revising them against observations. It then structures these hypotheses into typed objects and shared rules, simplifies their structure through category-theory-guided transformations, and formalizes the resulting model in Lean 4 to prove reachability and impossibility. AWM can formally prove that a goal is unreachable, even beyond explored trajectories. On two impossible game levels, it constructs closed, kernel-checked Lean proofs excluding every legal winning sequence in all six runs, while Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra, equipped with coding tools, produce none in twelve baseline runs. Four of AWM's six initial bounds are correct, compared with one for Opus 5.5 and none for GPT-6 Astra; given only feedback that a cheaper win exists, AWM revises its rules and constructs new proofs, reaching six of six correct bounds. Moreover, AWM produces interpretable mathematical models whose rules can be inspected and tested step by step. Across all 25 public ARC-AGI-3 games, these models enable Lean to replay the winning trajectories of all 183 levels and generalize beyond observed play, predicting unseen transitions and detecting changes in game mechanics. With Claude Opus 5, AWM clears all levels with a score of 99.9, while its certified winning trajectories use only 43% of the human baseline's actions.
