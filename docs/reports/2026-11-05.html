日本語要約 本論文は、LLMにおけるChain-of-Thought（CoT）推論のOOD一般化を、IDの原子的なステップを共有予測器で繰り返し合成する「構成的OOD一般化」として理論的に解析している。理論的には、最終タスクのリスクはステップ単位のIDリスクの和で制御され、推定誤差は共有によってTに線形ではなく概ね√(T/n)で増加することを示す。Atom-TaskとGSM8Kの実験でも、CoTによる短文から長文への一般化や構成的一般化が確認され、明示的な状態追跡の重要性が示された。

Tags: Chain-of-Thought, OOD generalization, theoretical analysis, LLM, Rademacher complexity

Abstract Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning substantially improves Large Language Models (LLMs), yet its out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization mechanism remains theoretically underexplored. We study CoT as compositional OOD generalization: the target task lacks complete reasoning trajectories or direct input-output pairs, while training provides only in-distribution (ID) atomic step data. We show that CoT generalizes by repeatedly applying a shared single-step predictor to compose reusable ID transitions. Theoretically, let $T$ denote the number of reasoning steps in the target task, $n$ the number of training samples per sub-task, and $k$ the number of output tokens per step; then the OOD task risk is controlled by the sum of the ID sub-task risks, scaled by a coefficient of order $\mathcal{O}(k)$ that is independent of $T$, although the summed risk itself may still grow with $T$. A Rademacher-complexity analysis further shows that the estimation term is of order $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T/n})$, rather than linear in $T$, due to predictor sharing under a uniform capacity control on the shared class. For autoregressive Next-Token Prediction, the final-task risk is bounded by the best-in-class step-level risk scaled by the same coefficient of order $\mathcal{O}(k)$, plus an estimation term of order $\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(k\sqrt{T/n}\big)$. A parity-coupled construction further shows that, for the sub-task empirical risk minimizer, the expected final-task risk is at least the best in-class risk plus $Ω(\sqrt{T/n})$ when $T\le n$. Experiments on Atom-Task and GSM8K support the theory: CoT enables short-to-long and compositional generalization, while explicit state tracking is crucial. Our code is available at https://github.com/maxliang114514/CoT-OOD.

Tags: LLM steering, closed-loop control, robust control, out-of-distribution, H-infinity

Abstract Brains and large language models need not be considered equivalent systems to reveal an interpretable commonality: both exhibit high-dimensional, nonlinear dynamics whose behavior can only be modeled imperfectly. This creates a shared challenge whenever we seek to intervene in their dynamics. A controller may either rely on a model identified around a particular operating regime and risk failure as the system moves away from that regime, or explicitly account for uncertainty and a changing internal state. Neurostimulation has confronted an analogous problem for decades. The limitations of fixed, open-loop stimulation have motivated closed-loop and state-dependent approaches that condition intervention on the ongoing state of the neural system. Inspired by this principle, we formulate LLM steering as a feedback-control problem in which the network's internal state, rather than a fixed intervention alone, determines how the system is perturbed. We further account for the mismatch between tractable dynamical models and the nonlinear transformer they approximate through robust control. As one principled realization of this framework, we develop an $H_\infty$-based steering controller that limits sensitivity to disturbances and linearization mismatches. Across multiple steering benchmarks, the controller improves performance over existing baselines, with particularly pronounced gains under out-of-distribution conditions and varying length complexities. Beyond controller performance, we introduce robust steerability, $S_{\mathrm{rob}}$, a pre-deployment measure of tolerance to dynamics mismatch; across model-task pairs, it exhibits task-specific structure and varies systematically with model scale. Beyond robust LLM steering, our measure asks not merely whether control is possible, but how much uncertainty it can survive; a principle that may span artificial and biological neural systems.

Abstract When many inputs share a budget of repeated attempts by a large language model (LLM), a common rule gives harder inputs more. On public math, code and formal-proof samples, the best published difficulty-proportional rule captures half or more of the oracle gain on benchmarks the model mostly solves, but at most 13% on hard workloads, even given true pass rates. Because the value of one more attempt peaks at intermediate difficulty, the optimal static allocation is inverted-U and gives little to inputs that are hopeless at the current budget.
